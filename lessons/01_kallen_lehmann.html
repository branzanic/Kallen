Lesson 1: The Källén–Lehmann Equation

Built from first principles — and why $Z\le1$ is a theorem, not an approximation

Equations are numbered throughout. The destination is Eq. \eqref{eq:kl}, which appears as Eq. (1) of the contribution.

1. What a propagator is

Create a disturbance in a field at spacetime point $y$; ask for the amplitude to find it at $x$. That amplitude is the propagator. For a scalar field it is the time-ordered two-point function,

\begin{equation} G(x-y) \;=\; \langle 0|\,T\,\phi(x)\,\phi(y)\,|0\rangle \label{eq:twopoint} \end{equation}

It depends on $x-y$ alone, not on $x$ and $y$ separately, because the vacuum is translation-invariant. That is already a physical statement, and it is what lets us Fourier transform to momentum space,

\begin{equation} G(k) \;=\; \int \mathrm{d}^4x\; e^{\,ikx}\,G(x) \label{eq:fourier} \end{equation}

where $k$ is a four-momentum and the Lorentz-invariant content sits in $k^2$.

2. The free field: one pole, residue one

For a non-interacting field of mass $m$, Eq. \eqref{eq:fourier} evaluates in closed form:

\begin{equation} G_0(k^2) \;=\; \frac{1}{k^2-m^2+i\epsilon} \label{eq:free} \end{equation}
How to read Eq. \eqref{eq:free}
  • The pole at $k^2=m^2$ says: there exists a particle of mass $m$.
  • The residue, here exactly $1$, says: the field operator $\phi$ creates that particle with full strength and creates nothing else.

Poles are what states exist. Residues are how strongly the field couples to them. Every idea in this lesson is a statement about residues.

3. What breaks when the field interacts

For a free field, $\phi(0)|0\rangle$ is a one-particle state and nothing more. Switch on an interaction and that stops being true: $\phi(0)|0\rangle$ becomes a superposition of every state carrying the right quantum numbers — one particle, two particles, bound states, all of it.

The exact $G$ is then not calculable in closed form. Källén (1952) and Lehmann (1954) asked what can be said about it regardless. The answer follows from that one observation, made precise.

4. The derivation

Move 1 — insert a complete set of states

The eigenstates of the Hamiltonian and momentum operators are complete:

\begin{equation} \mathbb{1} \;=\; |0\rangle\langle 0| \;+\; \sum_n \int\!\frac{\mathrm{d}^3p}{(2\pi)^3\,2E_p}\,|n,\mathbf{p}\rangle\langle n,\mathbf{p}| \label{eq:completeness} \end{equation}

The label $n$ runs over every multi-particle state the theory possesses. This is the entire physical input. Everything below is bookkeeping.

Move 2 — use translation invariance

Because $\phi(x)=e^{iPx}\phi(0)e^{-iPx}$, every matrix element carries its $x$-dependence in a phase:

\begin{equation} \langle 0|\phi(x)|n,\mathbf{p}\rangle \;=\; \langle 0|\phi(0)|n,\mathbf{p}\rangle\;e^{-ipx} \label{eq:transl} \end{equation}

The unknown dynamics is now confined to the numbers $\langle 0|\phi(0)|n\rangle$ — which we will never need to evaluate.

Move 3 — collect the unknowns into one function

Insert Eq. \eqref{eq:completeness} into Eq. \eqref{eq:twopoint} and use Eq. \eqref{eq:transl}. The positive-frequency part becomes

\begin{equation} \langle 0|\phi(x)\phi(0)|0\rangle \;=\; \sum_n \int\!\frac{\mathrm{d}^3p}{(2\pi)^3\,2E_p}\, \bigl|\langle 0|\phi(0)|n,\mathbf{p}\rangle\bigr|^2 e^{-ipx} \label{eq:wightman} \end{equation}

Lorentz invariance now does the decisive work: the squared matrix element can depend only on the invariant $p^2=m_n^2$, not on $\mathbf{p}$ separately. So define

\begin{equation} \rho(\kappa^2) \;=\; \sum_n (2\pi)\,\delta\!\left(\kappa^2-m_n^2\right) \bigl|\langle 0|\phi(0)|n\rangle\bigr|^2 \label{eq:rho} \end{equation}

Note what comes for free: $\rho \ge 0$ automatically, being a sum of squared moduli. Positivity is not an assumption.

Move 4 — recognise what each term is

With Eq. \eqref{eq:rho}, the sum over $n$ in Eq. \eqref{eq:wightman} becomes an integral over $\kappa^2$, and each slice is precisely the free propagator Eq. \eqref{eq:free} with $m\to\kappa$:

\begin{equation} G(k^2) \;=\; \int_0^\infty \mathrm{d}\kappa^2\; \rho(\kappa^2)\;\underbrace{\frac{1}{k^2-\kappa^2+i\epsilon}}_{G_0\ \text{of mass}\ \kappa} \label{eq:assemble} \end{equation}
The Källén–Lehmann representation
\begin{equation} G(k^2) \;=\; \int_0^\infty \mathrm{d}\kappa^2\; \frac{\rho(\kappa^2)}{k^2-\kappa^2+i\epsilon}, \qquad \rho(\kappa^2)\ \ge\ 0 \label{eq:kl} \end{equation}

The exact propagator of any interacting field is a superposition of free propagators. Non-perturbative, exact at any coupling, and derived from completeness alone. This is Eq. (1) of the contribution.

Four symbols, easily confused
  • $G$ — the propagator, the object represented.
  • $k^2$ — its argument: where you choose to evaluate $G$. Not a property of the system.
  • $\kappa^2$ — the integration variable: intermediate-state mass-squared. The poles of $G$ sit at $k^2=\kappa^2$.
  • $\rho$ — the spectral density.
  • $\epsilon$ — an infinitesimal, not a fifth physical quantity. It displaces each pole off the real axis so the integrand stays defined where $k^2=\kappa^2$, and it fixes the causal boundary condition; the residue at a pole is recovered as $\epsilon\to0^+$.

$k^2$ is where you look; $\kappa^2$ is what is there. In §7 this becomes the difference between $E_\mathrm{F}$ and the orbital energies.

You will meet that same infinitesimal written $i\eta$ in Eqs. \eqref{eq:lehmann} and \eqref{eq:yoshizawa} below. It is not a different object — the molecular literature simply needs another letter, because $\varepsilon$ is already taken by the orbital energy. For an isolated molecule nothing broadens the poles, and $E_\mathrm{F}$ sits in the gap far from all of them: no pole is approached, so $\eta\to0^+$ is indistinguishable from $\eta=0$, and $G_{rs}$ comes out real. Where a pole is approached — a molecule wired to leads, for instance — the infinitesimal is replaced by a finite broadening and the distinction stops being academic.

5. The shape of $\rho$, and why $Z\le1$

If the theory has a stable particle of mass $m$, that state contributes an isolated delta function; everything else contributes above the multi-particle threshold:

\begin{equation} \rho(\kappa^2) \;=\; Z\,\delta\!\left(\kappa^2-m^2\right) \;+\; \sigma(\kappa^2)\,\theta\!\left(\kappa^2-\kappa^2_{\text{thr}}\right) \label{eq:shape} \end{equation}

The equal-time canonical commutator $[\phi(\mathbf{x},t),\dot\phi(\mathbf{y},t)]=i\delta^3(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})$ fixes the total weight:

\begin{equation} \int_0^\infty \rho(\kappa^2)\,\mathrm{d}\kappa^2 \;=\; 1 \label{eq:sumrule} \end{equation}

Combining Eq. \eqref{eq:shape} with Eq. \eqref{eq:sumrule}:

\begin{equation} Z \;=\; 1-\int \sigma(\kappa^2)\,\mathrm{d}\kappa^2 \;\le\; 1 \label{eq:Z} \end{equation}
The constraint, in one sentence

The total spectral weight is fixed, so whatever the continuum takes, the one-particle pole loses. $Z$ measures how much free-particle character survives the interaction, and $Z=1$ if and only if $\sigma\equiv0$ — that is, only if the field does not interact.

$Z$ has a name — four of them

The same quantity is called different things depending on who is speaking, which is worth knowing because the literature does not translate itself:

The field-theoretic name is the literal one. In the LSZ reduction, $\sqrt{Z}$ is the factor relating the bare field operator to the asymptotic one-particle state, so $Z<1$ says the operator $\phi$ only partly creates a physical particle — the rest of its action lands in the continuum. In Fermi-liquid theory the same $Z$ is the size of the discontinuity in the momentum distribution at $k_\mathrm{F}$, and it controls the effective-mass enhancement.

So when a photoemission paper quotes a spectroscopic factor of $0.6$, it is quoting the molecular $Z$ — and asserting that $40\%$ of that ionisation's intensity is somewhere else.

The analytic structure follows at once: an isolated pole at $k^2=m^2$ with residue $Z<1$, plus a branch cut from $\kappa^2_{\text{thr}}$ to infinity. Poles are one-particle states; the cut is everything else.

6. The molecular version

Only Move 3 used relativity. Drop it, insert a complete set of $(N\!\pm\!1)$-electron eigenstates between field operators, and the same construction gives the one-particle Green's function of a molecule — Lehmann's representation:

\begin{equation} G_{rs}(E)\;=\;\sum_a \frac{\langle\Psi^N_0|a_r|\Psi^{N+1}_a\rangle\langle\Psi^{N+1}_a|a^\dagger_s|\Psi^N_0\rangle} {E-(E^{N+1}_a-E^N_0)+i\eta} \;+\;\sum_n \frac{\langle\Psi^N_0|a^\dagger_s|\Psi^{N-1}_n\rangle\langle\Psi^{N-1}_n|a_r|\Psi^N_0\rangle} {E+(E^{N-1}_n-E^N_0)-i\eta} \label{eq:lehmann} \end{equation}

The first sum is the addition half (electron affinities), the second the removal half (ionisations). The continuous density has collapsed into two discrete sums, because a finite system has a discrete spectrum.

Where the Dyson orbital comes from

The numerator of the removal half factorises. Define

\begin{equation} d^{\,n}(\mathbf{x}) \;=\; \sqrt{N}\!\int\! \Psi^{N-1\,*}_n\,\Psi^{N}_0\;\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_2\cdots\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_N \label{eq:dyson} \end{equation}

and the residue at pole $n$ is simply $d^{\,n}_r d^{\,n}_s$. So the Dyson orbital is the residue of the Källén–Lehmann propagator at an ionisation pole — not a one-electron orbital, but an overlap between two many-electron wavefunctions differing by one electron. Its squared norm is the molecular $Z$.

An electron-transfer coupling uses the removal half of Eq. \eqref{eq:lehmann} alone: the bridge mediates hole-like superexchange. That is a deliberate truncation of the representation, and worth stating as one.

7. What an orbital Green's function asserts

The expression actually evaluated in molecular-conduction work replaces the ionised states by orbitals:

\begin{equation} G_{rs}(E_\mathrm{F}) \;=\; \sum_k \frac{C_{rk}\,C_{sk}}{E_\mathrm{F}-\varepsilon_k+i\eta} \label{eq:yoshizawa} \end{equation}
This is Eq. \eqref{eq:kl} with the continuum deleted

$C_{rk}$ are coefficients of a normalised orbital, so every pole enters with residue $1$. And there are exactly as many poles as orbitals. In the language of Eq. \eqref{eq:shape}, Eq. \eqref{eq:yoshizawa} asserts

\begin{equation} \rho(\kappa^2) \;=\; \sum_k \delta\!\left(\kappa^2-\varepsilon_k\right), \qquad Z_k \equiv 1, \qquad \sigma \equiv 0 \label{eq:koopmans} \end{equation}

— which Eq. \eqref{eq:Z} says is the non-interacting case. This is the Koopmans limit: exact when one determinant describes the ionised state, and wrong in a definite, diagnosable way when correlation redistributes intensity.

What the failure looks like

Thiophene's $2b_1$ ($\pi_1$) ionisation near $12.5$ eV comes out at $\gamma=0.589$: it has given away $40\%$ of its weight. That weight is not lost — it reappears as two shake-up satellites at $\gamma=0.094$ and $0.131$. Those satellites are $\sigma(\kappa^2)$ of Eq. \eqref{eq:shape}, discretised. Eq. \eqref{eq:yoshizawa} has no poles at which to put them.

8. Two things to carry away

$Z\le1$ is a theorem, not an approximation

Eq. \eqref{eq:Z} follows from completeness and canonical commutation. Any interacting propagator spreads weight out of its pole. A method that assigns unit weight to every pole is not being slightly imprecise — it is asserting the system does not interact. Computing $\gamma_n$ turns that assumption into a result.

$k^2$ is not $\kappa^2$

The propagator is a function of an argument, distinct in character from the poles it possesses. In the molecular case: the poles are properties of the system, $E_\mathrm{F}$ is not — it is an input. A code that derives $E_\mathrm{F}$ from its own pole list has confused the two.
